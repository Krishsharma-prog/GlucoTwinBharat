01_intro
Welcome to GlucoTwin Bharat, an Indian meal-aware, personalized glucose Digital Twin proof of concept for the Happiest Health Digital Twin Challenge. In this walkthrough I will explain the problem, show how historical patient records and dynamic readings are combined, describe the forecasting model, and demonstrate the scenario-comparison workflow. We focus on a narrow target: forecasting glucose over the next two hours and estimating whether a configurable high-glucose threshold may be crossed. The prototype is built around React, a FastAPI backend, synthetic patient records, and machine-learning models in Python. Throughout this video, every patient and sensor record shown is fictional and generated for software demonstration. The results are not clinically validated, and the prototype must not be used for diagnosis, treatment, medication decisions, or emergency care. I will distinguish what is implemented from what would require further validation.

02_problem
The problem we are exploring is that a historical chart shows what has already happened, while a useful Digital Twin should update as new observations arrive and expose a forward-looking estimate. Glucose response is personal. Two people can respond differently to a similar meal because their medical history, current readings, sleep, activity, and other factors differ. This prototype does not claim to solve clinical glucose forecasting for real patients. Instead, it demonstrates the engineering pattern required to explore it: maintain a virtual patient, fuse separate sources of context, produce forecasts at specified horizons, and make the model inputs inspectable. The modeled outcome is a possible elevated-glucose excursion within one hundred and twenty minutes. The goal for a jury is to evaluate a working pipeline rather than a static user interface or an unsupported claim about medical accuracy.

03_twin
A Digital Twin is more than a dashboard or a single risk score. In this project, the virtual patient begins with a synthetic historical profile and a baseline glucose value. The twin also has a timestamped sequence of glucose and lifestyle observations. When a new simulated observation arrives, the current state changes. The forecast can then be recomputed using the updated values. This makes the system stateful: the same patient can have a different current state at a later timestamp. The dashboard exposes both the observed part of the timeline and the predicted part of the timeline. It also allows hypothetical inputs to be varied so the evaluator can compare outputs. These features demonstrate a Digital Twin workflow, while the simple synthetic response generator and the model remain engineering examples rather than a validated representation of human physiology.

04_meals
The project is designed around familiar Indian meal examples such as poha, idli, dosa, roti with dal, and rice with sabzi. These are scenario labels that make the interaction understandable to a local audience. The prototype asks for an explicit estimated carbohydrate amount rather than assuming that every plate of a dish has a fixed nutritional value. Portion size and recipe can vary substantially, so the values are illustrative parameters only. The meal selector is not providing nutrition advice. Its purpose is to let a reviewer change an input and see whether the software updates its forecast. That is a clear, testable use case: choose a fictional patient, enter a hypothetical meal, run the scenario, and inspect the difference from a baseline forecast. A scenario difference indicates model sensitivity under these assumptions; it does not prove that a real-world intervention would cause that change.

05_data
The dataset has two explicit streams. The first is the electronic health record, stored as a JSON file. It includes six fictional patients, age, sex, body mass index, a simulated diagnosis, a baseline glucose value, an illustrative HbA1c field, and individual simulation parameters. The second stream is a CSV time series with timestamped observations. It includes glucose, meal events and carbohydrate estimates, recent step count, sleep duration, and an activity label. The seeded observation data cover a day of five-minute intervals per virtual patient. There are no names of real patients, no imported clinical records, and no actual wearable connection. The application combines these streams at forecast time. That separation is important because historical profile information and the latest observed state have different meanings and should remain traceable in the architecture and documentation.

06_model
The forecasting module uses four Random Forest regression models, one for each horizon: fifteen, thirty, sixty, and one hundred and twenty minutes ahead. Training examples are generated from a transparent, stylized synthetic response process. Features include current glucose, a baseline value, synthetic HbA1c and BMI, age, estimated carbohydrates, time since meal, recent glucose slope, recent steps, sleep, and synthetic patient-specific sensitivity parameters. The output is a predicted glucose value for each horizon. The interface also reports the spread across trees and a model-only threshold-crossing signal. That signal is not a calibrated clinical probability. Random Forests were selected because they are relatively straightforward to train quickly and can be inspected and evaluated within this student-scale prototype. More complex models would not automatically make the product more reliable. External validation and good data quality would matter more.

07_state
Here is the twin update loop. First, the application loads the patient's historical profile. Second, it reads recent timestamped observations and derives a current glucose estimate, a recent trend, time since the last meal, and recent activity context. Third, the current virtual state is refreshed. Fourth, the model generates the next four forecast points. Fifth, those predictions are displayed alongside the observed series. Finally, a user can append another synthetic sensor point or compare a hypothetical meal scenario. The five-minute advance action changes the in-memory observation state, after which the current profile and forecast are refreshed. If the backend restarts, that simulated stream returns to the seed data because it is not persisted in a database. This is a deliberate simplification for a prototype. A production twin would require durable storage, timestamp validation, device connectivity, monitoring, and robust handling of missing or delayed sensor values.

08_architecture
The architecture has a React and Vite frontend, a Python FastAPI backend, and a local synthetic dataset. The frontend is responsible for patient selection, charts, scenario inputs, and showing model outputs. The API provides endpoints to retrieve the patient list, get one synthetic EHR record, read observations, append a simulated reading, request a forecast, compare a scenario, reset the demo, and review model metrics. The model code uses NumPy, pandas, and scikit-learn. Seed data and the source code are stored alongside the documentation for reproducibility. The application can be run locally without a paid cloud service. This is not a production deployment: authentication, hardened access controls, encrypted storage, and a real clinical database are not implemented. The local development API should not be exposed publicly with any real patient information.

09_dashboard
The dashboard brings the main pieces into one view. At the top are summary cards for the latest glucose observation, the sixty-minute estimate, the synthetic HbA1c value, and the model signal. The central chart shows the recent observed trajectory and a forward forecast section. A threshold line provides context for the excursion target. On the right, the patient profile makes the historical baseline visible. Lower panels summarize the dynamic stream, the forecast horizons, and the what-if controls. The purpose of this layout is transparency. A reviewer should be able to see which patient was selected, which information is historical, which values are current, and which numbers are model outputs. The visual dashboard is a walkthrough view of the data model and intended interaction. The submitted source includes the React frontend; the visuals in this generated narration are not a clinical product screenshot.

10_patient
For the patient example, we select the fictional identifier GTB zero zero two. The record includes an age and sex field, body mass index, a simulated type two diabetes description, a baseline glucose value, and illustrative HbA1c information. The observations table is separate from the EHR profile. Each row has a timestamp, a glucose value, recent steps, sleep duration, and optional meal information. This separation lets us ask whether the same baseline profile can be updated over time as the observation stream changes. The synthetic record is designed to exercise software behavior rather than represent a real person's medical history. No identifying details or real-world clinical data are present. In an actual clinical setting, access to health records would require appropriate authorization, privacy protection, access logging, data minimization, and clear rules governing how any derived prediction could be used.

11_forecast
After requesting the forecast, the backend returns points at fifteen, thirty, sixty, and one hundred and twenty minutes. The demo also displays the input context, including latest glucose, recent slope, meal carbohydrate amount, elapsed time since meal, steps, and sleep. In this captured example the forecast values are generated from the project's synthetic training process. They should be read as engineering outputs, not as health advice. The model's ensemble spread provides an additional descriptive signal, but it is not a full uncertainty interval and does not guarantee that the actual glucose value will fall within a particular range. The high-glucose threshold is configurable. Any system intended for clinical use would need a carefully justified target definition, calibration, subgroup error analysis, external evaluation, appropriate clinical oversight, and a documented plan for false positives and false negatives.

12_evaluation
The evaluation report compares each Random Forest forecast against a simple persistence baseline, which assumes the future value remains equal to the latest observed value. It reports mean absolute error and root mean squared error in milligrams per deciliter. The evaluation uses seven thousand two hundred synthetic training rows, one thousand eight hundred synthetic test rows, and twenty four held-out virtual patient identities. The split is grouped by virtual patient to reduce leakage between training and test partitions. In this generated dataset, the Random Forest has lower error than persistence at each horizon. That is useful evidence that the code pipeline can learn the synthetic generator's patterns and that the comparison metric is working. It is not clinical accuracy. Because the training and test targets share the same simulation assumptions, these numbers cannot establish real-world generalization. The report states that limitation directly.

13_scenario
The scenario lab lets the evaluator choose a meal, set an estimated carbohydrate quantity, and include recent activity and sleep values. The backend produces one baseline forecast with no newly specified meal and another forecast with the hypothetical scenario. It returns the predicted values at each horizon and a difference between them. In this example, the displayed numbers are produced by the same synthetic-trained model, using the virtual patient's current state. The important point is that the scenario is tied to that patient and that current data context; it is not just a universal response table. However, changing the model inputs does not prove causation. The difference does not show that a real meal choice or activity level would produce the same result for an actual person. The user interface therefore labels the comparison as a model-only what-if result and avoids treatment instructions.

14_api
The backend is inspectable through FastAPI's interactive documentation. A health endpoint reports service status and makes the synthetic-data mode explicit. The patient endpoints return the fictional patient list and one patient's historical profile. The observations endpoint returns recent time series records. The simulation endpoint adds a five-minute observation in memory. The forecast endpoint returns the predicted points and input context. The scenario endpoint returns both baseline and scenario forecasts. The metrics endpoint exposes the synthetic model evaluation. These endpoints make the workflow testable independently of the UI. The project's tests exercise the basic API behaviors, including profile retrieval, observation access, forecasting, scenario comparison, and simulated updates. Before external submission, the developer should run the clean setup steps, rerun the tests, check the public repository links, and verify the frontend on a machine with access to the required npm packages.

16_safety
Responsible use is central to this project. All seed patients and observations are synthetic. The application does not connect to a continuous glucose monitor and it does not ingest real health records. It is not validated for diagnosis, treatment, medication decisions, or emergency use. The ensemble frequency shown by the interface is uncalibrated and should not be described as a medical probability. The scenario comparison is not evidence that changing a meal or exercise pattern will cause a particular glucose outcome. There is no production authentication, authorization, audit system, or encrypted database. If the approach were developed further, it would need independent data, clinician review, prospective validation, security and privacy engineering, risk management, and appropriate regulatory review. In the current prototype, using synthetic data is the correct way to demonstrate the pipeline without exposing real people's health information.

17_roadmap
The next stage would start with better evidence, not a more complicated model. First, evaluate against an appropriately licensed and documented real-world research dataset, with participant-level separation between development and test data. Second, define the clinical target and operating thresholds with domain experts rather than choosing them only for visual effect. Third, measure calibration, performance across patient subgroups, missing-data behavior, and the consequences of false alerts. Fourth, add privacy controls, authentication, reliable storage, audit logging, and secure deployment practices. Fifth, plan a supervised prospective study before considering any clinical use. A future version could also integrate a real device stream, but only after authorization and data-quality checks. The current build is intentionally narrow. It demonstrates a technical foundation and a testable use case; it does not replace validation, medical supervision, or clinical judgment.

18_close
To conclude, GlucoTwin Bharat demonstrates a complete software concept for a meal-aware glucose Digital Twin. It fuses a synthetic historical profile with dynamic timestamped observations, updates a virtual patient state, predicts glucose at four future horizons, and compares hypothetical Indian-meal scenarios. It provides a dashboard concept, a FastAPI interface, synthetic evaluation metrics, reproducible seed files, tests, and documentation. Its most important contribution is not a claim of medical accuracy. It is the inspectable flow from historical records to a changing twin and then to an explicitly qualified forecast. The current limitations are equally important: there is no real sensor connection, no clinical dataset, no prospective validation, and no clinical calibration. Thank you for watching this walkthrough of GlucoTwin Bharat. The source code and documentation should be reviewed alongside the demo, and any real-world extension must begin with appropriate evidence, oversight, privacy protection, and safety.
